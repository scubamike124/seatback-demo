Airlines license entertainment by the cycle and program it by route, yet in-flight viewing data is patchy across fleets and arrives after the licences are bought. Seatback reads both ends of a route from Qloo's taste graph before you license, ranks the catalogue you can actually buy, and shows where else on your network the slate fits.

How it works, in four steps
  1. Read each city. Qloo's open location read shows what each end favours, and names its own top local artists and shows.
  2. Score your catalogue twice. Those local anchors score your titles in two independent readings. Titles the readings disagree on are withheld, not guessed.
  3. Balance the slate. Blend both ends by your passenger mix, keep variety, respect family-safe rules, and show the Qloo call behind every number.
  4. Extend it to your network. Qloo's cross-domain graph turns the slate's audience into the destinations it favours, checked twice, so you see which other routes the same licences suit.

What this slate does not tell you

  • THIS RUN USES SYNTHETIC FIXTURE DATA. No number on this page came from Qloo, and every title is labelled "(synthetic)".
  • Qloo measures the taste of audiences associated with a place or with anchor entities. It does not know who is on a given flight.
  • The passenger mix (origin share) is your assumption. Change it and the fit scores change.
  • Qloo does not know licence availability, cost or windows. Seatback ranks the catalogue you can license; "popular on this route" titles are leads to check.
  • Content ratings are US-style (MPAA and TV Parental Guidelines), as documented by Qloo. Airlines may apply their own edits and standards.
  • Ranks are relative within your catalogue. They are not viewing forecasts, and no in-flight viewing data was used.
  • Titles where two independent readings disagree are withheld, and a city whose readings disagree overall abstains rather than guessing.

Pilot and backtest plan (proposed, not run)

Nothing here has been validated against viewing data. This is what would test it with a real airline programmer.

  1. Backtest: take one past licensing cycle the programmer already closed. Run Seatback only on the catalogue that was available then, with the route mix they used.
  2. Compare, per route, Seatback's slate with what was programmed, on that cycle's route-level viewing hours per title. Pre-register the metric (for example, share of viewing hours captured by the top third of titles) before looking.
  3. Report it with its failures: routes where Seatback abstained, and routes where it did worse. Keep the route-blind baselines in the table.
  4. Pilot: one network segment for one cycle, Seatback slate on half of comparable routes and the usual slate on the rest. Success is a pre-agreed lift, with the programmer's own team judging it.
